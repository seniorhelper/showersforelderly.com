The short answer

A rolling shower chair only works if it can roll from the bedroom or toilet into the shower without a lip. What is a rolling shower chair, and how is it different from a shower commode chair?

A rolling shower chair is a waterproof seat on casters that a person sits in to be wheeled into the shower and washed. Most are built as shower commode chairs: the seat has an opening, so the same chair can roll over the toilet or hold a bucket. That's why families like them: one transfer in the bedroom instead of three in the bathroom.

There are two main builds. Attendant-propelled chairs have four small casters and are pushed by a caregiver; they're compact and turn tightly. Self-propelled chairs have large rear wheels the user can push; they need more width and more turning room. Some tilt back for people who can't sit upright, which changes both the size and the shower you need.

How do you measure the route?

  1. Bedroom and bathroom doors: measure the clear opening with the door open 90 degrees, from the face of the open door to the stop on the other side. Compare it with the chair's overall width from the maker's spec sheet. For a self-propelled chair, leave room for hands on the rims.
  2. Turns: measure the space where the chair has to turn into the bathroom and into the shower. Hallway-to-door turns stop more chairs than door widths do.
  3. Toilet: measure the toilet's rim height and compare it with the chair's seat height and clearance; the chair has to roll over the bowl, not into it.
  4. Shower entry: threshold height, entry width, and the inside depth with the chair and a caregiver in it.

Casters, brakes and seats: what to check

  • Locking casters: at least two should lock, and the locks should be reachable by the caregiver without kneeling on a wet floor.
  • Caster size: larger casters roll over grout lines and small transitions more easily; smaller ones turn tighter. Pick based on your floor, not the photo.
  • Seat: a padded, open-front seat makes washing easier; check the opening lines up with the toilet bowl.
  • Arms and footrests: flip-up or removable arms make side transfers possible; footrests should swing away so feet can be placed flat before standing.
  • Weight rating: read the maker's rated capacity and keep a margin.

Does Medicare pay for a rolling shower chair?

It depends on how it's prescribed and used, and the rules are narrow. CMS covers commodes "if a patient is bed- or room-confined" and physically unable to use regular toilet facilities; extra-wide heavy-duty commodes (HCPCS E0168) are covered for patients 300 pounds or more. Bath seats, on the other hand, appear on Medicare's coverage list as "Deny, comfort or convenience item; hygienic equipment" (NCD 280.1). A shower commode chair sits between the two, so ask the medical equipment supplier how they bill it before you assume coverage. When Part B does cover a commode, you pay 20% of the Medicare-approved amount after the Part B deductible.

When is it time for a roll-in shower instead?

If the chair can't get over the curb, can't make the turn, or the caregiver is kneeling outside a tub to wash someone, the chair isn't the problem. The shower is. Questions families ask

Can a rolling shower chair go into any walk-in shower?

No. It needs a shower with almost no threshold and enough room inside. The ADA caps roll-in shower thresholds at 1/2 inch and sets a 30 by 60 inch minimum size. A shower with a curb needs a ramp or a conversion to curbless.

What is the difference between a rolling shower chair and a shower commode chair?

Most rolling shower chairs are shower commode chairs: the seat has an opening so the chair can also roll over a toilet or hold a pail. Attendant-propelled models have four small casters; self-propelled models have large rear wheels and need more room.

How do I measure a doorway for a rolling shower chair?

Open the door fully and measure the clear opening from the face of the door to the stop on the other side. Compare it with the overall width on the chair maker spec sheet, leaving room for hands on the wheels of a self-propelled chair. Check the turn into the doorway too.

Does Medicare cover a rolling shower commode chair?

Sometimes. CMS covers commodes when a patient is bed- or room-confined and cannot use regular toilet facilities, while bath seats are denied as comfort or convenience items. Ask the supplier how the chair will be billed before buying.

Do rolling shower chairs need brakes?

Yes. At least two casters should lock, and the caregiver should be able to reach the locks easily. Lock the chair before every transfer and before washing.
