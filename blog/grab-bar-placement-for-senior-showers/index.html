What Should You Know First?

Useful grab-bar placement starts with the person’s real entry, turning, sitting, standing, and washing sequence. Bar location, orientation, gripping surface, wall backing, fasteners, and nearby controls must work together; a bar mounted in the wrong place may be sturdy yet difficult to use.

This guide is for older adults, families, occupational therapists, and remodelers planning permanent shower supports. It uses plain language, real planning questions, and current primary references. It does not assume that one product, threshold, or layout is best for every user.

01

Observe before locating a bar

Watch the dry movement sequence with appropriate supervision. Note where the person pauses, reaches, turns, lowers to a seat, and rises. Handedness, joint range, vision, balance, and mobility equipment influence which support positions feel natural.

02

Plan the entry support

Entry support should help with the actual threshold and approach without forcing a long reach or blocking the opening. Door panels, curtains, nearby fixtures, and the wet-to-dry transition all affect usable placement.

03

Coordinate support with seating

A seated user may need support for controlled lowering, upright posture, and standing. The bar must relate to seat height, depth, armrests, available wall length, and the direction of transfer.

04

Compare horizontal, vertical, and angled bars

Orientation changes how the hand and arm load the bar. Vertical bars can assist at a transition, horizontal bars offer support along a path, and angled bars may follow a rise. Choose from observed movement rather than appearance.

05

Provide structural backing

A grab bar transfers load into the wall structure. Plan blocking or an approved mounting system before walls close, follow the bar and fastener instructions, and coordinate penetrations with the waterproofing system.

06

Keep controls and spray within reach

A user should not release support and lean across the shower to operate the valve or handheld spray. Lay out bars, controls, hose, shelves, and seat together so one feature does not defeat another.

07

Choose a usable gripping surface

Diameter, texture, clearance from the wall, wrist comfort, and wet grip matter. Decorative rails and towel bars are not substitutes. Test a comparable grip if arthritis, weakness, or limited hand closure is involved.

08

Avoid conflicts and false confidence

A bar can conflict with a folding seat, sliding door, shower curtain, soap dish, or caregiver position. It also cannot correct a slippery floor, difficult threshold, poor lighting, or an unsafe transfer method.

09

Plan for more than one user

Households may include people with different heights, reach, and abilities. Look for placements that support the primary user without creating a projection or obstacle for others, and document why each location was chosen.

10

Inspect after installation

Confirm the correct model, location, fasteners, wall connection, and finish. Follow applicable inspection and manufacturer guidance. Recheck for movement, corrosion, loose escutcheons, or damaged sealant over time.

11

Know when clinical input helps

An occupational or physical therapist can evaluate complex transfers, one-sided weakness, progressive conditions, or caregiver technique. Their movement assessment can translate functional needs into a better construction plan.
